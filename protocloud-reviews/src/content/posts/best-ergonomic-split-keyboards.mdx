A standard keyboard asks your hands to do three unnatural things at once: turn outward at the wrist to meet a flat board, pull together in front of your chest, and lay flat with palms down. Do that for a decade and something complains. An ergonomic keyboard undoes one or more of those. These five range from a board you can switch to over lunch to one that takes a month and changes how you type for good.

## The three adjustments, in order of impact

**Split.** Two halves, placed shoulder width apart, so your arms hang straight instead of angling in. This is the single biggest fix and every board here does some version of it.

**Tenting.** Raising the inner edge of each half so your palms face slightly inward, like holding a cup. Reduces forearm twist. Available on three of these boards, optional on one.

**Columnar stagger.** Keys arranged in straight vertical columns instead of the diagonal offset inherited from typewriters. Fingers move straight up and down. Big benefit, big relearn.

Pick the fewest adjustments that solve your problem. If your wrists hurt, split alone may do it. If your forearms hurt, add tenting. If your pinkies hurt, look at columnar boards with thumb clusters.

The K860 is the gateway. A single curved board with the keys gently split and tented, a padded wrist rest that is actually comfortable, and a layout close enough to normal that most people type at full speed by the end of day one. It pairs with three devices and works with the locked-down laptop your IT department gave you.

The keys are membrane, not mechanical, so it feels like a good laptop keyboard rather than a typist's instrument. The footprint is large. For a lot of people with early wrist discomfort, it is all they need.

<ProductCard index={1} {...frontmatter.products[1]} />

The Freestyle Pro is two halves of a normal mechanical keyboard on a tether. Put them exactly where your shoulders are. Add the tenting kit and angle them. Cherry MX Brown switches and a standard layout mean you lose no speed on day one. Everything is programmable.

Kinesis has made this board for years and it looks it. The tenting kit should be in the box and is not. It is wired. None of that matters when your wrists stop hurting.

The Voyager is where the serious ergonomic crowd has landed. Two tiny halves with low-profile switches, keys in straight columns, and a thumb cluster for Space, Enter, Backspace and layer keys. Magnetic legs tent it. It fits in a laptop sleeve next to the laptop, which no other board here manages.

Fifty-two keys means numbers and symbols live on layers. Expect two weeks of slow typing and a month before it feels natural. After that, most people cannot go back. ZSA's configurator is the best in the business and the firmware is open source.

<ProductCard index={3} {...frontmatter.products[3]} />

The Moonlander is the Voyager's larger sibling with full-height switches, a bigger thumb cluster, and tenting built into the legs. The thumb cluster is the point: it moves Enter, Space, Backspace, Tab and modifiers to your strongest digit and off your weakest.

Some hands love the cluster and some find the far keys a stretch. The board is expensive and large. If your pinky and ring finger are the problem, it solves it more completely than anything else here.

The Q11 is for people who want to split their keyboard and change nothing else. It is a standard 75 percent layout cut in two, in a heavy aluminium case, with hot-swap switches and knobs on each half. Place the halves apart and type exactly as you always have.

No tenting, no columnar layout, no learning curve. It is heavy and wired. For a first split board that will not cost you a week of productivity, it is the obvious pick.

## How to adopt a split board without quitting

- Start with the halves close together and move them apart over a week.
- Keep your old keyboard plugged in for the first few days for deadlines.
- Do a typing tutor for ten minutes a day on a columnar board. It halves the adjustment time.
- Expect your accuracy to drop before it recovers. That is normal.

## The short version

- Try ergonomics without a learning curve: Logitech Ergo K860.
- Split a mechanical board, standard layout: Kinesis Freestyle Pro.
- Portable, columnar, serious: ZSA Voyager.
- Pinkies hurt, all in: ZSA Moonlander.
- Split but otherwise normal: Keychron Q11.
