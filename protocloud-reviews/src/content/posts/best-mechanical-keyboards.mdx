A keyboard is the most used tool a programmer owns and the one most people never think about. Our team has gone deep on this. Between us we have typed on everything from gasket-mounted aluminium slabs to a 60 percent board with no arrow keys. These six are the ones that stayed on desks.

## Layout first, switches second

Most buying guides lead with switches. We think that is backwards. Layout decides how your hands move all day.

**75 percent** keeps the function row and arrow keys in a compact body. It is the sweet spot for most programmers, and four of our picks use it.

**60 percent** drops arrows and the function row onto layers. Vim and tmux users love it. Everyone else fights it for a week and then either converts or returns it.

**Full size** matters if you enter numbers all day or you share the board with spreadsheets. Otherwise it is wasted desk space.

On switches, the [full explainer](/mechanical-keyboard-switches-explained) goes deep. The short version: linear is smooth and quiet, tactile has a bump you feel, clicky has a bump you hear and your colleagues hear too. Hall effect switches use a magnet instead of a metal contact, which lets you set how far a key travels before it registers. For gaming that is huge. For typing it is a nice-to-have that also means the switch never wears out.

The Q1 HE is the keyboard we recommend when someone says "I want one good board and I do not want to think about this again." The aluminium case is heavy and dead silent. The gasket mount gives a soft, bouncy feel. The Hall effect switches let you dial the actuation point so light that typing feels like gliding, or so deep that typos drop to nothing.

Remapping happens in a browser. Mac and Windows layouts switch with a toggle. The battery lasts weeks with the backlight off.

It is expensive, and at over two kilograms it is furniture. If you move between desks, look at the K2 HE below.

<ProductCard index={1} {...frontmatter.products[1]} />

Same switches, same software, same layout, in a lighter plastic-and-wood case for a lot less money. The K2 HE is what we hand to someone trying their first mechanical keyboard. It is very hard to be disappointed.

The case has a little flex if you press on it. You will not notice while typing. Stock keycaps are fine and easy to replace if you catch the hobby.

If you have spent years on a laptop keyboard and full-height mechanical switches feel like climbing stairs, the Air75 V2 is the bridge. The travel is short, the switches are hot-swappable, and the board is thin enough to sit over a MacBook's own keyboard with NuPhy's optional feet.

It travels well. It pairs with everything. The feel is unlike a full-size mechanical, and some people never adjust, so try one before committing if you can.

<ProductCard index={3} {...frontmatter.products[3]} />

The HHKB is a cult object and the cult is right. Topre switches are not mechanical in the strict sense. They use a rubber dome over a conical spring and a capacitive sensor, which produces a deep, rounded "thock" that nothing else quite matches.

The layout is the point. Control sits where Caps Lock usually does, Backspace is one row lower, and the arrows live on a layer under the right hand. If you spend your day in a terminal and an editor with modal keybindings, it feels like the board was designed for you. If you spend your day in spreadsheets, it will drive you mad.

Expensive, yes. People keep them for fifteen years.

<ProductCard index={4} {...frontmatter.products[4]} />

Sometimes you need a keyboard that works with a locked-down corporate laptop, pairs with three devices, and does not announce itself. The MX Mechanical does all of that. The low-profile switches are quiet enough for an open office, the backlight turns on when your hands approach, and the Logi Bolt receiver is reliable in a way Bluetooth alone is not.

It is not an enthusiast board. You cannot swap switches and the feel is mild. It is the sensible choice, and sometimes sensible is the brief.

<ProductCard index={5} {...frontmatter.products[5]} />

Wooting's 80HE exists because competitive gamers wanted the lowest possible input latency, and it turns out that engineering is pleasant to type on too. Polling at 8 kHz, adjustable actuation, and rapid trigger, which resets a key the instant you start lifting it. The analog mode turns WASD into a joystick.

For writing code alone, this is more than you need. For an engineer who games in the evening on the same desk, it replaces two keyboards. Wired only, and stock is often tight.

## If your wrists hurt

None of the boards above is ergonomic in the split or tented sense. If you have pain rather than preference, start with our [ergonomic and split keyboards guide](/best-ergonomic-split-keyboards) instead.

## Switch recommendations, briefly

- Open office or shared home: linear or silent tactile.
- You want feedback without noise: tactile.
- You work alone and like the sound: clicky, and good luck on calls.
- You are curious about Hall effect: start with the K2 HE and set a shallow actuation.

## The short version

- One board, no regrets: Keychron Q1 HE.
- First mechanical keyboard: Keychron K2 HE.
- Laptop feel, better keys: NuPhy Air75 V2.
- Terminal lifer: HHKB Hybrid Type-S.
- Corporate laptop, three devices: Logitech MX Mechanical.
- Code by day, game by night: Wooting 80HE.
