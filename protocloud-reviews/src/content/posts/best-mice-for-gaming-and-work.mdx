A lot of engineers have two mice: a heavy, button-laden productivity mouse by day and a light gaming mouse by night. That is one mouse too many. Modern gaming mice are light enough to be comfortable through a full workday, and a few have the extra controls that make editor and browser work faster. These eight do both jobs well, from flagship mice to budget picks that cost far less. If you never game, our [productivity mouse guide](/blog/best-mice-for-productivity) has the ergonomic picks.

## How we picked

These picks come from manufacturer specifications, professional reviews and owner reports, not our own hands-on testing. The first four are premium mice; the last four cost less and make a few more compromises. Shape matters more than any spec: ergonomic mice suit a relaxed palm grip, small symmetrical ones suit claw and fingertip grips.

## What crosses over

**Weight.** The best thing gaming did for mice was make them light. A 60 g mouse causes less wrist fatigue over a day than a 140 g one. This benefits everyone.

**Sensor and latency.** Reviewers rate the tracking and responsiveness of every mouse here highly. For work, that shows up as a cursor that goes exactly where you meant, every time.

**Battery.** Gaming mice went from charging nightly to charging monthly. Wireless is now the default.

**Buttons.** This is where the two uses diverge. Competitive mice strip buttons for weight. Productivity wants a thumb wheel and a few extras. Only a few mice here have them.

## Premium picks

<ProductCard index={0} {...frontmatter.products[0]} />

The Superlight 2 is a long-standing favourite of professional players, and the same traits make it an excellent office mouse. At around 60 grams your wrist barely notices it. The shape is a safe medium that suits palm and claw grips. The battery lasts weeks, and owners report the wireless is as reliable as a cable.

What you give up is buttons: two side buttons and a scroll wheel. There is no thumb wheel for horizontal scroll, no free-spin for long files. If you lean on those, look at the G502 X Plus. If you do not, it covers work and games on its own.

<ProductCard index={1} {...frontmatter.products[1]} />

The Viper V3 Pro is a few grams lighter still and has a shape that fingertip and claw grippers tend to prefer: lower, flatter, with a gentle hump at the back. Reviewers praise the tracking, battery is long, and owners describe the symmetric body as balanced.

Side buttons are on the left only, so it is right-handed in practice despite the shape. Razer's software has little to offer for productivity. As a plain pointing device for all day and all evening, it has few weaknesses.

<ProductCard index={2} {...frontmatter.products[2]} />

The G502 X Plus is the one gaming mouse that is also a serious productivity mouse. Thirteen programmable controls, including a thumb-side sniper button that is ideal for push-to-talk or a modifier, and the scroll wheel toggles between notched and free-spin for flying through a long log. The sculpted shape fills a palm grip comfortably.

It is heavy by current standards. Over 100 grams is fine for most people and tiring for some. It is right-handed only and comes with lighting you will switch off. Among the premium picks, it has by far the most buttons.

<ProductCard index={3} {...frontmatter.products[3]} />

Lamzu made its name selling flagship-grade mice for far less, and the Atlantis Mini Pro is the one we recommend. Around 50 grams, a top-tier PixArt sensor, solid wireless and a comfortable symmetrical shape.

The software is basic and the Mini size suits medium hands best. Larger hands should look at the standard Atlantis. The shell has small cutouts that some people like and some do not.

## Budget picks

You do not need a flagship to play well. The sensors in budget mice now track as accurately as anyone can aim, and good wireless has stopped meaning lag. What you give up as you spend less is weight, polling rate, build and battery.

<ProductCard index={4} {...frontmatter.products[4]} />

The G305 has been the default answer to "which cheap wireless gaming mouse?" for years, and it still earns it. Logitech's Lightspeed wireless is as responsive as a cable, the HERO sensor tracks precisely, and one AA battery lasts up to 250 hours of play, or months in Logitech's low-power Endurance mode. The small symmetrical shape suits claw and fingertip grips, and the receiver stores inside the mouse for travel.

The trade-offs show its age. It takes an AA battery instead of charging over USB-C, the 1,000 Hz polling rate is behind newer mice, and the plain shell has no lighting. None of that matters much in play, which is why it remains one of the most-reviewed gaming mice on Amazon.

<ProductCard index={5} {...frontmatter.products[5]} />

The Mercury X Pro is the budget pick for competitive players. At 49 grams it is the lightest mouse in this guide, its magnesium frame is open to keep your hand cool, and it pairs PixArt's top-tier PAW3950 sensor with an 8,000 Hz dongle in the box. You can also connect over Bluetooth for a laptop, or plug it in.

The skeleton design is not for everyone: some people love the look and the airflow, others dislike the feel of holes under their fingers, and it collects dust. There are only five buttons, and GravaStar is a smaller brand, so its owner reviews number in the hundreds rather than the thousands.

<ProductCard index={6} {...frontmatter.products[6]} />

If you play MMOs or games with a lot of binds, or just want a mouse that fills your hand, look at the Basilisk V3. Its sculpted right-hand shape has a proper thumb rest, and eleven programmable buttons cover push-to-talk, pings and abilities. The HyperScroll wheel switches between notched scrolling and free-spin, which is as handy for long code files and documents as it is for cycling weapons.

It is wired, right-handed only, and at around 101 grams it is heavier than the wireless picks. Think of it as the budget G502 X Plus. For palm grippers who want buttons and comfort over raw speed, those are fair trades.

<ProductCard index={7} {...frontmatter.products[7]} />

The M612 is a lot of gaming mouse for little money. It has eleven programmable buttons, including two side macro keys and a rapid-fire button, five DPI presets you can change on the fly, and a grippy textured side. More than 11,000 owners have rated it, and the average is solid.

It is the heaviest mouse in this guide, at about 126 grams, and Redragon does not say which sensor it uses. As a first gaming mouse, a spare, or a mouse for a second PC, it is good value.

## A note on mouse pads

A light mouse on a bare desk or a tiny pad is wasted. A large cloth pad gives consistent glide and lets you lower your sensitivity, which improves precision for both code and games. It is the cheapest upgrade in this entire category.
