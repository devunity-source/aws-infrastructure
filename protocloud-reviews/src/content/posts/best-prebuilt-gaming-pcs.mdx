A prebuilt gaming PC is no longer the overpriced shortcut it used to be. Graphics card prices settled, assemblers buy parts in bulk, and the gap between a prebuilt and the same parts bought separately is now small. For a lot of engineers it is also a useful second machine: the graphics card that runs games at 1440p also runs local AI models, and eight fast cores chew through Docker builds and test suites. These seven cover everything from a budget 1080p box to a 4K machine.

## How we picked

We have not tested these towers ourselves; we picked them by their parts lists. A prebuilt is the sum of a CPU, a graphics card, memory, storage, a power supply and a cooler, and each of those is a known quantity. We favoured listings that name their parts, use a power supply rated 80+ Bronze or better, ship with at least 16 GB of memory and an NVMe SSD, and have a meaningful number of owner reviews. Where a listing contradicts itself, we say so in the card.

Every machine here comes with Windows 11 and a one-year warranty. None needs assembling.

**What matters most, in order:**

- **Graphics card.** It decides your resolution and frame rate more than anything else. An RTX 5060 or 5060 Ti suits 1080p and lighter 1440p. An RTX 5070 or RX 9070 XT is a real 1440p card. An RTX 5070 Ti is where 4K gets comfortable.
- **Video memory.** 8 GB is now the floor and already tight in some new games. 12 GB is comfortable. 16 GB leaves headroom and is what you want for running local language models.
- **CPU.** AMD's X3D chips, with extra cache stacked on top, are the fastest gaming CPUs you can buy. For compiling and general work, any modern 8-core is plenty.
- **Platform.** AM5 boards will take new Ryzen CPUs for years. AM4 and Intel's LGA 1700 are finished, which matters only if you plan to upgrade the CPU later.

The Azure pairs the two parts we would pick if building from scratch: a Ryzen 7 9850X3D, the fastest gaming CPU in this guide, and a Radeon RX 9070 XT with 16 GB of video memory. That card comes close to NVIDIA's RTX 5070 Ti in ordinary games and drives a 1440p high-refresh monitor comfortably. The 360 mm liquid cooler keeps the X3D chip at full boost.

It also has more owner reviews than every other machine here put together, and they are strong. That counts for a lot with prebuilts, where build quality and shipping damage vary between assemblers.

The compromises are small. Wi-Fi is the older Wi-Fi 5 standard, so use Ethernet if you can. Memory runs at 5600 rather than the 6000 that Ryzen prefers, which costs a few percent at most. And in games that lean heavily on ray tracing, NVIDIA still leads, which is where the next pick comes in.

## Plug the monitor into the graphics card

The most common first-day problem with any desktop that has a graphics card: the monitor is plugged into the video ports on the motherboard, near the top of the back panel, and shows nothing. Use the horizontal row of ports lower down, on the graphics card itself. Every machine here outputs over DisplayPort and HDMI. While you are setting up, pair the tower with something worth looking at; our [monitor guide](/blog/best-monitors-for-coding) covers 4K and ultrawide options.

## What to upgrade first

- **RAM to 32 GB** on the 16 GB machines. It is the cheapest upgrade and the one you will notice in every game and browser.
- **A second SSD.** Games now take 100 GB or more each. Most of these boards have a free M.2 slot.
- **The keyboard and mouse.** The bundled ones work, but they are the cheapest part of the box. See our picks for [mice that work for gaming and the office](/blog/best-mice-for-gaming-and-work) and [mechanical keyboards](/blog/best-mechanical-keyboards).
- **Ethernet over Wi-Fi.** For online games, a cable beats any wireless standard.
