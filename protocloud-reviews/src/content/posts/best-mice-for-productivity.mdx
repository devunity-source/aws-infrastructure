Wrist pain is the occupational injury nobody warns you about when you start writing software. A good mouse does not fix a bad desk, but a bad mouse will find any weakness you have. These five have put in long hours across our team. Two of them are not mice at all.

## What actually matters

**Shape before sensor.** Every mouse here tracks perfectly. The question is whether your hand sits on it in a neutral position or claws around it. If you have ever ended a day with a tight forearm, that is the clue.

**Scroll wheel quality.** Programmers scroll constantly. A wheel that is free-spinning when you want to fly and notched when you want control saves real effort over a day. Logitech's electromagnetic wheel is still the best implementation.

**Multi-device switching.** If you have a work laptop and a personal machine on the same desk, a mouse that hops between them with a button saves a receiver and a lot of fumbling.

The MX Master 4 is the fourth version of the mouse half of the tech industry already uses, and the changes are the right ones. The thumb panel now has haptic feedback, so a tap pulls up a context ring without looking down. The scroll wheel still switches from ratchet to free-spin on its own when you flick it. The thumb wheel handles horizontal scrolling in a wide terminal or swaps between desktops.

It is a big, heavy mouse. For precise cursor work that can feel sluggish. For eight hours of editors, browsers and terminals, the weight reads as stability. Right-handed only, as ever.

<ProductCard index={1} {...frontmatter.products[1]} />

The Anywhere 3S is the Master for people who work from laptops and cafes. It is small, it tracks on a glass table, and it has the same scroll wheel. We keep one in the bag and a Master on the desk.

For a full day at a desk, most of us find it too small. Hands cramp around it. That is the trade for the size.

A vertical mouse rotates your hand so the forearm bones sit parallel, the way they do when you shake hands. For people with early RSI symptoms it can be the difference between working and not. The Lift is the one we recommend first because it is lighter than most verticals, it comes in a left-handed version, and the clicks are quiet.

Expect a few days of clumsy cursor work while your brain recalibrates. If your hands are large, Logitech's MX Vertical is the same idea at a bigger size.

<ProductCard index={3} {...frontmatter.products[3]} />

Two engineers on our team moved to trackballs and will not go back. The pitch is simple: your arm does not move at all. The cursor is driven by fingertips on a ball, and the scroll ring sits around it. Once it clicks, after about a week, it is fast and almost effortless.

The Expert Mouse is the big-ball version, ambidextrous, with a wrist rest that snaps on. It needs a wipe every couple of weeks. It will probably outlast the desk it sits on.

<ProductCard index={4} {...frontmatter.products[4]} />

If you are on a Mac and you have ever flicked between full-screen apps with four fingers, you already know why the trackpad is here. Gestures are faster than any button for window and desktop management, and there is no wrist rotation at all. Several of us run a trackpad on the left and a mouse on the right.

It is a poor tool for pixel-precise selection and it is effectively Mac only. For everything else about moving around a Mac, nothing beats it.

## A note on wrist rests and pads

A soft gel rest under the wrist can make a pain problem worse by compressing the carpal tunnel. If you use one, rest the heel of the palm on it, not the wrist. A large, low-friction cloth pad does more for comfort than any wrist rest.

## The short version

- The default: Logitech MX Master 4.
- Lives in a bag: Logitech MX Anywhere 3S.
- Wrist is complaining: Logitech Lift.
- Want to stop moving your arm entirely: Kensington Expert Mouse.
- Mac gestures are your workflow: Apple Magic Trackpad.
