Wrist pain is the occupational injury nobody warns you about when you start writing software. A good mouse does not fix a bad desk, but a bad mouse will find any weakness you have. These six are built for long hours. Two of them are not mice at all.

## What matters

**Shape before sensor.** Reviewers rate the tracking on every mouse here highly. The question is whether your hand sits on it in a neutral position or claws around it. If you have ever ended a day with a tight forearm, that is the clue.

**Scroll wheel quality.** Programmers scroll constantly. A wheel that is free-spinning when you want to fly and notched when you want control saves real effort over a day. Logitech's electromagnetic wheel is the best-known version of this.

**Multi-device switching.** If you have a work laptop and a personal machine on the same desk, a mouse that hops between them with a button saves a receiver and a lot of fumbling.

The MX Master 4 is the fourth version of a mouse that many engineers already use, and the changes are practical ones. The thumb panel now has haptic feedback, so a tap pulls up a context ring without looking down. The scroll wheel still switches from ratchet to free-spin on its own when you flick it. The thumb wheel handles horizontal scrolling in a wide terminal or swaps between desktops.

It is a big, heavy mouse. For precise cursor work that can feel sluggish. For eight hours of editors, browsers and terminals, the weight reads as stability. Right-handed only, as ever.

<ProductCard index={1} {...frontmatter.products[1]} />

The Anywhere 3S is the Master for people who work from laptops and cafes. It is small, it tracks on a glass table, and it has the same scroll wheel. A common setup is one in the bag and a Master on the desk.

For a full day at a desk, many owners find it too small, and larger hands cramp around it.

A vertical mouse rotates your hand so the forearm bones sit parallel, the way they do when you shake hands. For people with early RSI symptoms it can help a great deal. The Lift is the one we recommend first because it is lighter than most verticals, it comes in a left-handed version, and the clicks are quiet.

Expect a few days of clumsy cursor work while your brain recalibrates. If your hands are large, Logitech's MX Vertical is the same idea at a bigger size.

<ProductCard index={3} {...frontmatter.products[3]} />

Many people who switch to a trackball do not go back. The appeal is that your arm does not move at all. The cursor is driven by fingertips on a ball, and the scroll ring sits around it. Once it clicks, after about a week, it is fast and takes very little effort.

The Expert Mouse is the big-ball version, ambidextrous, with a wrist rest that snaps on. It needs a wipe every couple of weeks, and it should last a decade.

<ProductCard index={4} {...frontmatter.products[4]} />

If you are on a Mac and you have ever flicked between full-screen apps with four fingers, you already know why the trackpad is here. Gestures are faster than any button for window and desktop management, and there is no wrist rotation at all. Some people run a trackpad on the left and a mouse on the right.

It is a poor tool for pixel-precise selection and it is effectively Mac only. For everything else, gestures are the quickest way around a Mac.

<ProductCard index={5} {...frontmatter.products[5]} />

If you want the MX Master's best tricks without its price, the TECKNET gets surprisingly close. It has a side scroll wheel for wide spreadsheets and timelines, a metal main wheel, and it switches between three devices: one on the USB dongle and two over Bluetooth. The clicks are silent, which colleagues on calls will appreciate, and TECKNET rates the battery at about three months per charge with light daily use.

The compromises are in the details. There is no software to remap the buttons, the side buttons do not work on a Mac, and the shape is a comfortable generic one rather than the sculpted support of the Master or the Lift. As a first upgrade from a laptop trackpad or a basic office mouse, it is good value.

## A note on wrist rests and pads

A soft gel rest under the wrist can make a pain problem worse by compressing the carpal tunnel. If you use one, rest the heel of the palm on it, not the wrist. A large, low-friction cloth pad does more for comfort than any wrist rest.
